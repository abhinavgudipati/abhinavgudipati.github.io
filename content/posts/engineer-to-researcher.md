When I left NatWest to join ISB as a Research Associate, I thought the transition from software engineering to academic research would be straightforward. I was wrong in ways I didn't anticipate — and right in ways that surprised me.

## What transfers directly

The most underrated skill I brought from engineering is comfort with ambiguity in data. At NatWest, I worked with financial data that was incomplete, inconsistently formatted, and often wrong in subtle ways. You learn to distrust your inputs and verify obsessively.

In research, this is exactly the disposition you need. Empirical finance is littered with data quality issues — survivorship bias, look-ahead bias, stale prices, corporate action adjustments done wrong. Engineers think about edge cases; researchers should too, but not all of them do.

Programming is the other obvious transfer. Being comfortable with Python and SQL puts you miles ahead in an empirical research setting. You can move from question to answer much faster than someone who has to learn the tooling alongside the method.

## What doesn't transfer

The hardest adjustment has been the pace and the feedback loop.

In engineering, you ship. You write code, it runs (or it doesn't), you fix it, you deploy. Feedback is fast. Progress is visible. There's a satisfying linearity to it.

Research is the opposite. You can spend three weeks on a methodology question and end up exactly where you started. You run a regression and the result is ambiguous — not wrong, just ambiguous. You reframe the question and try again.

> The absence of a merge request to close is disorienting at first. The work doesn't end; it deepens.

The other thing that doesn't transfer is the relationship with expertise. In engineering, expertise is largely additive — you learn a new framework or language and your capability grows measurably. In research, expertise is more about taste and judgment. Knowing the right question to ask is harder to acquire, and harder to measure, than knowing how to use pandas.

## What I wish I'd known

Start reading papers earlier. I wish I'd spent the last year at NatWest reading one finance paper a week. The gap between knowing how to work with data and knowing what questions are worth asking is enormous, and papers are how you close it.

Find the researchers who write clearly. Not all academic writing is impenetrable — there are finance researchers who write with real clarity and make their ideas accessible. Those papers will shape how you think about structuring arguments.

Don't underestimate how much domain knowledge matters. I came in strong on the quantitative side but weak on the institutional knowledge of how markets actually work — the rules, the actors, the incentives. That gap took longer to close than I expected.

## A year in

I don't regret the switch. The problems are harder, the stakes feel different, and there's something genuinely exciting about working on questions that don't have known answers yet.

But it is a different kind of work. If you're thinking about making a similar move, go in with eyes open — and don't stop writing code.
