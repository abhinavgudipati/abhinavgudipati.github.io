The conventional path to a finance PhD in the US or UK looks something like this: strong undergraduate degree, possibly a master's, maybe a research assistantship, then applications. The timeline is tight and linear.

I took a different route — two years in industry, then a research associate position at ISB before applying. This wasn't the plan from the beginning, but looking back, I think the detour made my application stronger and my research instincts sharper.

## The industry years

At NatWest, I worked on data engineering problems that were genuinely large in scale. The systems I worked on touched hundreds of millions of pounds worth of operations. That's not something you get exposure to in a research environment, and it gave me a visceral sense of how financial institutions actually work — not in the textbook way, but in the messy, legacy-system, regulatory-constraint way.

That context turned out to be more useful than I expected. When I read papers on bank risk management or financial contagion now, I have a mental model of how decisions actually get made inside these institutions. That's not a small thing.

## Why not apply directly?

Honestly, I wasn't ready. I knew I wanted to work in empirical finance, but I didn't have a clear research agenda or any exposure to how academic research actually works. Applying to PhD programs without those things is a weak position to be in.

The research associate role at ISB gave me something concrete: real papers to work on, a mentor who has navigated the academic system, and time to figure out what questions I actually find interesting.

> An RA position is the closest thing to a trial run of the PhD. You get to experience the work before committing to five or six years of it.

## What the ISB year taught me

Working with Prof. Thirumalai has been clarifying in the best way. He asks hard questions — not to be difficult, but because the hard questions are where the interesting work lives. I've learned more about empirical methodology in this year than I did in two years of reading papers on my own.

I've also learned what I don't know. My accounting knowledge was weak when I arrived. My understanding of the institutional details of Indian capital markets was surface-level. Both of those gaps are closing, but they're closing because I was forced to confront them — not because I had time to study them abstractly.

## Would I recommend this path?

It depends on what you want. If you're confident in your research interests and already have exposure to the research process, going directly to a PhD program is probably the right call. The opportunity cost of two or three years is real.

But if you're like me — curious, quantitatively capable, but not yet sure what kind of researcher you want to be — a stint in industry followed by an RA position is a legitimate path. You arrive at the PhD application with real experience, real references, and a much clearer sense of what you're getting into.

The goal is still the PhD. The route just looks a bit different.
